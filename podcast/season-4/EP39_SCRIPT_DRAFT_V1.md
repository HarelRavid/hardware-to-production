# SPOKEN SCRIPT

## Opening

Three suppliers receive what appears to be the same RFQ.

Same part number.

Same drawing.

Same quantity.

Same delivery date.

All three return reasonable quotations.

Supplier A assumes the material is supplied in one condition.

Supplier B assumes a different heat-treatment route.

Supplier C plans to subcontract a critical process.

One supplier includes full dimensional inspection.

Another assumes only critical dimensions need reporting.

A third includes packaging that would be completely unacceptable for the surface finish.

Nobody is necessarily incompetent.

The problem is that the RFQ did not define the same technical problem for all three suppliers.

That is the first lesson of this episode.

An RFQ is not only a commercial document.

For hardware, it is also an engineering interface.

And if that interface is ambiguous, the quotation may look precise while the technical assumptions underneath it are completely different.

So this episode asks:

**What must a supplier understand and prove before “they made a good sample” becomes credible first-production approval?**

By the end, I want you to be able to build two things:

A technically complete RFQ package.

And a First Production Approval Matrix that tells you what evidence is actually needed before release.

---

## 1. The RFQ should make competent suppliers solve the same problem

A useful RFQ starts with configuration.

Which drawing revision?

Which model?

Which BOM or specification?

Which approved material condition?

Which interfaces?

Which customer or regulatory constraints matter?

What is optional?

What is forbidden?

What is still open for supplier proposal?

This does not mean dumping every internal document into an email.

The purpose is to remove assumptions that could change cost, process, quality or evidence.

Think of it this way:

If two competent suppliers can read the package and reasonably quote two fundamentally different manufacturing routes, then either that flexibility is intentional—or the technical package is incomplete.

Sometimes you want supplier creativity.

That is fine.

But define where the supplier is free to propose.

And define where the product claim requires control.

---

## 2. A drawing is necessary—and often not sufficient

Mechanical teams sometimes assume:

“If the drawing is correct, the RFQ is correct.”

But supplier production depends on more than geometry.

Material.

Surface condition.

Special processes.

Cleaning.

Heat treatment.

Inspection.

Test.

Traceability.

Packaging.

Tooling.

Software or test requirements.

Certificates.

Approved sub-tier processes.

Handling.

Change notification.

Some of those belong on the drawing.

Some belong in specifications.

Some belong in the purchase requirement.

Some belong in a supplier quality agreement.

The exact architecture depends on your system.

The important thing is that the supplier sees one coherent technical definition.

A technically ambiguous RFQ can create a dangerous illusion:

the quotation is comparable,

but the evidence burden is not.

---

## 3. CTQs and acceptance need to be visible

Suppose the supplier is making the Sentinel Node enclosure.

The drawing contains thirty dimensions.

But three characteristics strongly affect the product claim.

Mounting datum position.

Connector interface geometry.

Sealing land flatness.

Those do not necessarily need a giant red “CTQ” stamp in every company.

The naming method varies.

But the supplier should understand which characteristics matter functionally, which process risks matter, and how acceptance will be demonstrated.

That includes the measurement expectation.

If you ask for a tolerance but never discuss how it will be measured, you may discover later that supplier and customer use different methods.

Same part.

Different result.

The RFQ is where those assumptions should become visible before the first dispute.

---

## 4. Material and process requirements need the right level of freedom

There is a balancing act here.

Overspecify the process and you can trap the supplier into your own immature manufacturing assumptions.

Underspecify it and the supplier may choose a route that does not preserve the product evidence.

If the product really requires a defined alloy, condition, heat treatment, plating, cleanliness level or process family, state it.

If the requirement is actually functional and multiple processes are acceptable, define the functional outcome and let the supplier propose.

But if the supplier changes a process variable that can change the claim, that is not “just their internal method.”

It becomes part of the approval envelope.

That is the recurring idea from this entire podcast:

Evidence is valid inside an applicability envelope.

Supplier approval is the same.

---

## 5. What does first-production approval actually ask?

Now the parts arrive.

They look good.

Dimensions pass.

Can we approve the supplier?

Maybe.

But first ask what the evidence actually represented.

Was the part made at the real production site?

With production-intent material?

With the real tooling?

The real process route?

The real sub-tier special process?

The real inspection method?

The real operator or production conditions?

At a representative rate?

Was this a hand-selected engineering sample or the actual process we intend to release?

That is the central question.

**What did the evidence represent?**

A beautiful part made by a prototype route may be strong design evidence.

It may be weak production evidence.

---

## 6. First Article Inspection — one specific evidence model

Aerospace has a formal First Article Inspection framework under IAQG 9102.

IAQG describes 9102 as a standardized FAI process and documentation approach for aviation, space and defense product, usable across supply-chain levels.

It can also be adopted elsewhere where a standardized FAI process is useful.

The key transfer lesson is not:

“Every supplier needs aerospace paperwork.”

The lesson is:

A first article is a deliberate verification of a defined product/configuration under a defined manufacturing context.

And first-article evidence is tied to that state.

The exact contractual requirement depends on sector and customer.

So if you are not in aerospace, do not casually say:

“We need AS9102.”

Ask what evidence question you are trying to answer.

Then choose the method that fits your obligation and risk.

---

## 7. PPAP — a different evidence question

Automotive uses Production Part Approval Process—PPAP.

At the public level, AIAG describes PPAP as a production-part approval method intended to establish that engineering design-record and specification requirements can be consistently met using actual production processes at production rates.

That is a broader production-process approval question than simply saying:

“Here is one conforming first article.”

Again, the exact submission levels, records, triggers and customer-specific requirements are not universal.

And I am not going to teach them from memory.

But the conceptual distinction is extremely useful.

First article asks one kind of evidence question.

Production process approval asks another.

Sustained capability asks another.

Do not collapse them.

---

## 8. FAI is not PPAP, and neither one is sustained capability

Let's make the distinction explicit.

Question one:

**Did the defined first-production/configuration output conform to the specified characteristics?**

That is the kind of question associated with first-article verification.

Question two:

**Has the representative production process and its required evidence been accepted for production?**

That is the broader production-part/process approval question.

Question three:

**Does the process continue to behave acceptably over time, volume, maintenance, material lots, operators and change?**

That is sustained production capability.

Those are different levels of evidence.

A conforming first article can be important.

It is not proof that the line can sustain rate.

A successful approval package can be important.

It is not proof that nothing will ever drift.

That is why Episode 40 exists.

---

## 9. Certificates are evidence objects—not magic documents

Many supplier packages include certificates.

Material certificates.

Certificates of conformance.

Process certificates.

Calibration records.

These can be valuable.

But the right question is:

What claim does this document support?

Which lot?

Which process?

Which part?

Which revision?

Which requirement?

A certificate that cannot be tied to the actual production evidence may be weak.

A certificate that is clearly linked to a requirement, source and affected population can be exactly the right evidence object.

So avoid both extremes.

Do not worship paperwork.

Do not dismiss it.

Connect it to the claim.

---

## 10. The First Production Approval Matrix

Here is the listener tool.

For every important approval claim, create one row.

Claim.

What evidence is needed?

Was the evidence generated with representative source/process/configuration?

What is the result?

Is there a deviation or open gap?

Who owns the decision?

What is the release status?

What change would trigger re-review?

Let's use Sentinel Node.

Claim:

Enclosure sealing interface meets the drawing and functional requirement.

Evidence:

Dimensional results for sealing land.

Material identity.

Surface/process records where relevant.

Representative production tool/process.

Functional sealing verification.

Measurement method.

Supplier/site identity.

If the enclosure moves later from CNC bridge production to molding, the old approval does not automatically transfer.

The evidence dependency changed.

That is why the matrix includes the reapproval trigger.

---

## 11. Tooling, NRE and ownership

RFQs also fail when tooling ownership is unclear.

Who owns the mold?

Who owns the fixture?

Who owns the inspection program?

Who owns the test software?

Can the tooling move?

Who maintains it?

What happens at end of life?

These sound commercial.

They are also technical continuity questions.

If production recovery depends on a tool that cannot legally or physically move to another source, that is part of the supply risk.

If the supplier owns undocumented test software that defines acceptance, that is part of the evidence risk.

So make ownership visible before the purchase order turns ambiguity into dependency.

---

## 12. Deviations and substitutions

No real supplier relationship stays perfect forever.

A material becomes unavailable.

A sub-tier changes.

The supplier proposes an alternate process.

A dimensional deviation appears.

The wrong approach is to handle every exception in disconnected email.

The useful question is:

Does the deviation or substitution change the evidence supporting the product claim?

What population is affected?

What re-verification is needed?

Is the change temporary?

What is the effectivity?

How will the as-built population be reconstructed later?

That is where the RFQ/approval system connects back to configuration management.

---

## 13. DEV to LVP to serial production

In DEV, supplier samples may be exactly what you need.

You are learning.

Testing geometry.

Exploring process routes.

Comparing materials.

Do not pretend those samples prove production readiness if they do not.

At LVP, representative first-production evidence becomes much more important.

The supplier, process, tooling, measurement and traceability need enough maturity to support repeat builds.

At serial production, approval state has to survive change.

New site.

Tool rebuild.

Material source.

Sub-tier process.

Capacity expansion.

Inspection change.

The evidence system has to know when the old approval still applies and when it does not.

---

## 14. Six traps to avoid

Trap one:

**Drawing-only RFQ is technically complete.**

Sometimes it is enough.

Often it is not.

Trap two:

**One good sample means the supplier is production approved.**

Not necessarily.

Trap three:

**FAI and PPAP are basically the same.**

They answer different evidence questions and have different applicability.

Trap four:

**PPAP is a universal hardware requirement.**

It is not.

Trap five:

**A certificate proves the part is good.**

Only relative to the claim and traceability it actually supports.

Trap six:

**Supplier approval never expires.**

Approval remains meaningful only inside the evidence envelope that was demonstrated.

---

## 15. The action after this episode

Take one active RFQ.

Ask a simple question.

What could two competent suppliers interpret differently?

Material?

Process?

Inspection?

Packaging?

Tooling?

Traceability?

Sub-tier?

Change authority?

Add only the information required to make those assumptions explicit.

The goal is not a bigger RFQ.

It is a cleaner engineering interface.

---

## Closing

Episode 39 gets us to first-production approval.

But real production begins after the approval.

Suppliers change.

Demand changes.

Sub-tiers change.

Tools wear.

Plants transfer.

Materials become unavailable.

A “backup supplier” exists on a spreadsheet but has never built the current revision.

So the next question is:

**How do you keep supplier capability trustworthy after the original approval package is complete?**

That is Episode 40.

Supplier Quality, Dual Sourcing and Resilience.

Because a second supplier name is not the same thing as a second qualified production system.
