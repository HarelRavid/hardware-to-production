# SPOKEN SCRIPT

## Opening

A supplier makes ten prototype parts.

They are excellent.

Dimensions are good.

Surface finish is good.

Delivery is on time.

The engineering team is happy.

Then ramp begins.

And defects appear.

The supplier moved production to a different machine.

A different sub-tier provides one critical operation.

The inspection method changed.

The operator skill changed.

The material lot is different.

Nothing on the drawing changed.

The sample was good.

The production source was never validated as a system.

That is the difference this episode is about.

A supplier can be capable of making samples.

That does not automatically mean the source is ready to support production ramp.

By the end, I want you to be able to ask:

**Which exact external production system are we relying on—and what evidence says it can support our configuration, quality and rate?**

---

## 1. Prototype supplier and production source are different evidence states

In development, a flexible prototype supplier can be perfect.

Fast.

Creative.

Willing to make three parts.

Able to improvise.

That is valuable.

But the production question is different.

Will the ramp parts come from the same process?

Same site?

Same tooling?

Same material source?

Same sub-tier?

Same inspection method?

Same test method?

Same operator skill?

Same documentation?

If not, the sample proves only what the sample route proved.

That is not a criticism of the supplier.

It is an applicability boundary.

A sample can be excellent design evidence and weak source-readiness evidence at the same time.

---

## 2. Identify the production-source system

For a critical supplied part, identify the source system.

Supplier.

Site.

Process route.

Machine or equipment family where it matters.

Tooling.

Critical material source.

Sub-tier special process.

Inspection and measurement.

Packaging and handling where relevant.

Production revision and approved deviations.

You do not need every internal supplier parameter.

You need the identity that affects the product/process claim.

This is the same principle we used inside our own factory:

Definition.

As-built/as-made state.

Evidence.

The company boundary does not remove the need for traceability.

---

## 3. Capability and measurement are already part of supplier readiness

Wave 02 covered the quality side.

Can the supplier measure the important characteristics?

Is the source process stable/capable enough for the decision?

What is the first-production approval evidence?

What changes require impact review?

We do not need to reteach those tools here.

Instead, during ramp ask:

Is that quality evidence still representative of the source actually producing the ramp volume?

A measurement study on one inspection station may not apply to a different station.

A process capability study on one line may not apply to a transferred line.

Supplier readiness is not a folder of old approval records.

It is current evidence tied to current production state.

---

## 4. Quoted capacity is not demonstrated supplier capacity

This is where procurement and operations often talk past each other.

A supplier says:

“Our machine can make 1,000 parts per day.”

What does that mean?

Machine theoretical cycle rate?

One shift?

Three shifts?

Dedicated machine or shared capacity?

Material available?

Operators available?

Inspection capacity?

Yield?

Changeover?

Maintenance?

Sub-tier capacity?

Packaging?

Shipping cutoff?

A machine rate is not source capacity.

Use the same logic from Episode 29.

What accepted output can the supplier system sustain under the conditions we will actually depend on?

If the supplier must make 600 good parts per day, but inspection can release only 400, the machine's 1,000-per-day headline does not solve the problem.

If one sub-tier heat-treatment batch creates a three-day queue, machine rate is not the constraint.

If 10 percent rework returns to the same grinder, capacity is lower than gross output.

Supplier capacity is a system claim too.

---

## 5. Incoming inspection can be useful—and still be the wrong long-term control

Suppose a source process is unstable.

The customer responds with 100 percent incoming inspection.

That can be a sensible containment action.

It may protect production now.

But ask whether incoming inspection can actually see the failure mechanism.

Surface dimension?

Maybe.

Internal material state?

Maybe not.

Latent weld quality?

Depends on the method.

Software configuration?

Different problem.

The long-term supplier-readiness question is:

What evidence controls the mechanism at the source?

Incoming inspection is one control.

It is not a universal substitute for source-process control.

This is exactly the nuance we locked in Wave 02.

---

## 6. Contract manufacturer: who owns what?

Contract manufacturing creates another risk.

Everybody assumes somebody else owns the problem.

Who owns process definition?

Who owns tooling maintenance?

Who owns test software?

Who owns calibration?

Who owns rework instructions?

Who owns the quality data?

Who decides whether a deviation is acceptable?

Who approves engineering changes?

Who controls supplier substitutions?

Who maintains the genealogy?

There is no universal responsibility matrix.

The solution is not one standard RACI chart.

The solution is explicit ownership.

If a product fails test and the CM patches test software to recover the unit, who approves the patch?

If a fixture wears and shifts measurement, who detects it?

If the CM wants to move production to another line, who decides what evidence is needed?

These questions belong in the technical/commercial interface before ramp pressure arrives.

---

## 7. Change during ramp needs effectivity across company boundaries

A supplier proposes an alternate material.

Or a site transfer.

Or a new machine.

Or a different sub-tier.

The purchasing system might see:

“same part number.”

Engineering should see:

“possible evidence-envelope change.”

Not every supplier change requires full requalification.

But consequential changes need impact assessment.

Which claims depend on the changed attribute?

What evidence remains valid?

What new evidence is needed?

Which purchase orders/lots receive the change?

What is the effectivity boundary?

How will old and new populations be identified?

That is configuration management at the supply chain boundary.

---

## 8. Build the Supplier Readiness Card

Here is the listener tool.

For one critical part or process, record:

Product/process claim.

Source and site identity.

Production process.

Tooling.

Technical package revision.

Capability evidence.

Measurement/inspection method.

Capacity and lead time.

Quality history.

Change control.

Traceability and sub-tier.

Recovery plan.

Release status.

Then ask:

What evidence is current?

What is assumed?

What came from a prototype route?

What came from production-intent conditions?

That is the supplier-ramp gap.

---

## 9. Supplier development is a ramp activity

Sometimes the source is not ready.

That does not always mean replace the supplier.

Maybe the supplier needs:

A better fixture.

Clearer CTQs.

Measurement support.

Process-control improvements.

Work-instruction clarification.

Capacity balancing.

Sub-tier control.

A more realistic technical package.

Supplier development can be the right intervention.

But it should be driven by a defined gap.

Not vague pressure to “improve quality.”

The goal is demonstrated production capability.

Not supplier theater.

---

## 10. DEV to LVP to ramp

In DEV, fast prototype suppliers and substitutions can be rational.

If the evidence limit is visible.

In LVP, production-intent source identity becomes important.

You need enough control to learn from repeated lots.

During ramp, source capability and capacity become commercial constraints.

Quality, lead time, change discipline and recovery have to work together.

The supplier has become part of your factory.

Even if the building belongs to someone else.

---

## 11. Five traps to avoid

Perfect prototype parts mean production-ready supplier.

No.

Supplier machine rate equals supplier capacity.

No.

Incoming inspection can compensate forever for unstable source process.

No.

The CM owns everything because they build the product.

No.

Commercial purchase terms automatically define technical change authority.

No.

---

## 12. The action after this episode

Pick one critical supplier.

Ask them one question:

**Which exact process, site, tooling and inspection route will make our ramp parts?**

Then compare that answer to the evidence used to approve them.

Any mismatch is a readiness question.
