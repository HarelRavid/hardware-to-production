## Listener transformation
Before: frames the choice as either “move fast and improvise” or “build a mature factory too early.”
After: distinguishes legitimate learning shortcuts, bridge production routes and minimum controlled production modes from hidden manufacturing debt, and gives every shortcut an explicit expiration condition and migration path.

## Narrative hook
A process works at small scale. The team prepares to increase throughput by roughly fifty times — and water vapor that was harmless before starts clogging the process lines.

The opening question is:

> When does “it works” stop being transferable evidence simply because we need more of it?

## Teaching flow
1. AIMM: small-scale process proof does not prove the same process architecture at much higher throughput.
2. Establish scale as an applicability change: flow, heat, byproducts, residence time, handling, queueing and control behavior may change.
3. Prixel Press: low-NRE laser cutting, vacuum forming, 3D printing and desktop CNC are legitimate bridge routes when their purpose and expiration are visible.
4. Reuse the canonical Shortcut Expiration logic: temporary does not mean uncontrolled.
5. Bedrock Sandals: supplier loss leads to a deliberately engineered small in-house mode — layout, ergonomics, safety and scheduling still matter.
6. Introduce MINIMUM CONTROLLED PRODUCTION MODE: smallest viable production system that still protects consequential safety/quality/configuration/release obligations.
7. Free Form Fibers: an R&D apparatus becomes a production-system redesign problem involving technician access, flow, quality points and data capture — not merely a faster machine.
8. Close with the startup rule: defer sophistication where evidence allows; never defer visibility of the risk, configuration or expiration trigger.

## Core framework — Manufacturing Debt Register
Reuse the existing prototype-shortcut concept:
`Shortcut → value today → claim supported → claim not supported → risk introduced → controls retained → volume/risk envelope → expiration trigger → next route → migration lead time → evidence needed before cut-in`.

Do not create a new startup maturity model.

## Case spine
### Case 1 — AIMM
Owns: scale-dependent process mechanism and evidence-transfer failure.
Guardrail: the approximate 50× scale target is case context, not a universal revalidation threshold.

### Case 2 — Prixel Press
Owns: bridge manufacturing and planned route migration.
Guardrail: maker-space processes and contract manufacturing are options, not prescribed destinations.

### Case 3 — Bedrock Sandals
Owns: constrained/disrupted production and minimum controlled production mode.
Guardrail: footwear risk/context differs from high-hazard hardware; transfer the control logic, not the shop configuration.

### Case 4 — Free Form Fibers
Owns: R&D equipment → production-system architecture transition.
Guardrail: specialized process; use the access/data/quality lesson rather than generalizing the equipment solution.

## Listener tool — Startup Manufacturing Debt Register
For every temporary choice:
`Why are we doing this now? → what uncertainty does it retire? → what future claim can it not support? → what is the observable expiration signal? → how long does the replacement route take? → what evidence must exist before migration?`.

## DEV / LVP / SVP
DEV: optimize for learning speed while recording assumptions/configuration and keeping production claims narrow.
LVP: manual/bridge production can be professional if hazards, CTQs, configuration, test, nonconformance and release are controlled proportionately.
SVP: bridge routes expire when rate, cost per good unit, process variation, supportability or traceability exceed their demonstrated envelope.

## Prototype shortcut / expiration
Shortcut: low-NRE prototype/bridge process, manual fixture, R&D equipment or constrained in-house mode.
Expires when: the process no longer supports the required claim, risk level, sustainable throughput, quality, cost or recovery behavior.

## Common mistakes
- automating or buying enterprise infrastructure to look mature;
- keeping a prototype route because it has “always worked” despite changed scale;
- calling manual production uncontrolled by definition;
- discovering the replacement route only after the bridge route breaks;
- treating supplier loss as justification to abandon release discipline;
- confusing cash-efficient deferral with unowned risk.
