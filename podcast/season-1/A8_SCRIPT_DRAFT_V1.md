# SPOKEN SCRIPT

## Opening

Two prototypes sit on the bench.

From the outside, they look identical.

One passed the environmental test.
One failed.

At first, that sounds useful.
You have a difference. Now you can investigate it.

Except a few weeks later, the team discovers that the two units were not actually the same.

One had a different connector lot.

One was running a newer firmware build.

One had been hand reworked after assembly.

The calibration table was different.

And somewhere during the week, the environmental test procedure had also been edited.

Now what exactly did the failure teach you?

Maybe the product changed.
Maybe the process changed.
Maybe the software changed.
Maybe the test changed.

Or maybe several things changed at once.

The problem is not that the team iterated quickly.

The problem is that it lost the ability to reconstruct what actually happened.

That is configuration management.

And if the phrase makes you imagine a giant PLM implementation, a change-control board and six forms before you are allowed to move a screw hole, that is exactly the misconception I want to remove in this episode.

For an early hardware team, configuration management starts with a much simpler question:

**If this unit passes or fails today, can we reconstruct exactly what we built, tested, changed and reworked six weeks from now?**

By the end of this episode, I want you to be able to create one lightweight configuration record for your next meaningful prototype — and test your current system by trying to reconstruct your last five builds.

---

## 1. Configuration management is product truth, not software

A hardware product is never just a CAD file.

It can include mechanical drawings.
PCB revisions.
BOMs.
Firmware.
Calibration data.
Supplier choices.
Materials.
Test procedures.
Work instructions.
Tooling and process definitions.

Which of those matter depends on the product and the claim you are trying to support.

But the principle is stable:

If changing an item can change the meaning of a test result, the quality of a unit, the safety of the product, the service procedure or the population affected by a problem, its identity may need to be controlled.

You do not need enterprise software to begin doing that.

In early development, a spreadsheet, serial label, drawing revision, BOM snapshot and software build ID may be completely adequate.

The important thing is not the platform.

The important thing is whether the team can answer:

What was supposed to be built?

What was actually built?

What changed?

Which units received the change?

And which evidence belongs to which state?

When those questions become difficult, configuration debt is accumulating whether or not you own expensive PLM software.

---

## 2. Three different truths

I want to separate three things that hardware teams often collapse into one.

**Definition.**

What should we build?

That may include the released drawing, BOM, firmware release, specification, approved process or other controlled definition.

**As-built — or as-programmed, as-configured, as-run.**

What did this particular unit or build actually become?

And then:

**Evidence.**

What happened when that state was inspected, tested or used?

Those three things are connected.
But they are not the same object.

Imagine the BOM says sensor A.

During a shortage, somebody installs sensor B under a temporary approval.

The drawing still looks the same.
The enclosure still looks the same.
The part fits.

But that unit's as-built state is now different from the default definition.

If sensor B changes noise, calibration or temperature behavior, the evidence generated by that unit may no longer mean the same thing.

Or take firmware.

The physical unit does not change at all.
But if the calibration algorithm changes, the product state changed in a way that may matter to performance evidence.

Configuration management is how you prevent those changes from becoming invisible.

---

## 3. What is the minimum identity a prototype needs?

Do not answer this by collecting everything.

More traceability is not automatically better traceability.

The right question is:

**Which facts would we need later to understand a consequential pass, fail, change or field problem?**

For a typical electro-mechanical prototype, that might include:

A unique unit or build ID.

Mechanical revision.

PCB or hardware revision.

BOM revision and important approved alternates.

Firmware or software build.

Relevant calibration or configuration data.

Critical material, supplier or lot information where it can affect the claim.

Test procedure version.

Links to results.

And important deviations or rework.

That is not a universal mandatory list.

For one product, supplier lot may be critical.
For another, it may add no useful value.

For one test, firmware version may completely change the conclusion.
For a purely mechanical dimensional inspection, it may be irrelevant.

The goal is reconstructability appropriate to the risk and the evidence.

Not collecting data because it might look mature on a dashboard later.

---

## 4. Rework adds history

Here is a pattern that destroys useful evidence surprisingly quickly.

A unit fails.

The engineer opens it.
Moves a connector.
Adds a shim.
Changes a wire.
Touches up a solder joint.
Updates a parameter.

The unit passes.

And the final record says:

PASS.

That record has erased the most interesting part of the story.

A better record is not necessarily complicated.

Initial failure.
Observed condition.
Rework or deviation.
Resulting configuration.
Retest.
Final result.

Why keep that history?

Because later you may discover that the rework itself explains a reliability issue.

Or that every passing prototype had the same hidden manual adjustment that never made it into the production definition.

Or that the operator in production cannot reproduce the expert engineer's recovery step.

A final PASS does not make the previous failure irrelevant.

It tells you the unit eventually reached a passing state.

Engineering needs to know how.

Now, this does not mean every cosmetic touch-up on an early bench article needs a formal enterprise nonconformance workflow.

Use judgement.

The rule is simpler:

If the change or rework could alter the engineering conclusion, preserve it.

---

## 5. Revision and effectivity are different questions

Suppose drawing revision C is released on Monday.

What revision is the product?

The easy answer is Rev C.

But what about:

Parts already in WIP?

A supplier shipment built against Rev B?

A work instruction that was not updated yet?

A firmware build paired with the old hardware?

A temporary deviation allowing old inventory to be used for twenty more units?

This is where a useful distinction appears.

**Revision** asks:
What definition exists?

**Effectivity** asks:
Where, when and for which units or lots does that definition actually apply?

Releasing a revision is not the same as proving it was implemented everywhere you intended.

In a small startup, effectivity might simply be:

“Serial number 025 onward.”

“Production order 17 onward.”

“Use new part after old lot is depleted.”

“Temporary deviation for ten units while qualification continues.”

The mechanism matters more than the vocabulary.

You need to know where the old state ends and the new state begins.

Otherwise a field failure six months later becomes a detective story.

---

## 6. Change control is not just approving the change

Hardware teams often think the change process ends when someone says yes.

But approval only answers:

Should we make this change?

It does not prove that the change was implemented correctly.

A useful change sequence looks more like this:

What is changing?

Why?

What else could it affect?

What evidence do we need?

Who approves it?

Which definitions change?

Where does the change become effective?

Did production, suppliers, software, test and documentation actually cut over correctly?

And which units contain the old versus new state?

That is why configuration management and verification are connected.

A change is not finished simply because the new drawing exists.

It is finished when the intended configuration is implemented and the evidence required by the impact is complete enough for the decision you are making.

---

## 7. The Change Impact Check

When something changes, ask eight questions.

**One: What exactly changed?**

Not “the sensor.”
Which manufacturer, part, revision, firmware coefficient, material, process or geometry changed?

**Two: Which units or builds receive the change?**

Where is the cut-in?

**Three: Which requirements, interfaces or critical characteristics depend on it?**

**Four: Which existing evidence depended on the old state?**

**Five: Which supplier, process, tooling or test assumptions changed with it?**

**Six: What new evidence is proportionate?**

Maybe analysis is enough.
Maybe a focused regression test.
Maybe a pilot lot.
Maybe broader requalification.

Do not start from “retest everything.”
And do not start from “it is only a small change.”

Start from the dependency.

**Seven: How is effectivity recorded?**

Which units are old and which are new?

**Eight: Can we reconstruct both populations later?**

If the answer is no, you may be creating a future investigation problem even if today's change works perfectly.

This eight-question check is our practical tool for the series.
It is not a substitute for whatever formal change process your industry or customer may require.

## 8. Sentinel Node: the “drop-in” sensor that may not be drop-in

Let's return to the Sentinel Node.

The vibration sensor becomes supply constrained during ramp planning.

Purchasing finds an alternate.

Same package.
Similar measurement range.
Headline specifications look close.

The easy change is:

Update the ERP item.
Build one unit.
Run a quick functional test.
Move on.

But what claims might actually depend on the sensor?

Sensitivity.
Frequency response.
Noise.
Temperature drift.
Power.
Firmware driver behavior.
Calibration coefficients.
Production test limits.
Mechanical loading.
EMC behavior.
Reliability.
Supplier process quality.

Not every one of those will necessarily matter.

That is exactly the point.

The team should not automatically repeat every test ever performed.

It should identify which claims and evidence depend on the characteristics that changed.

Maybe the dimensional review is trivial.
Maybe a bench comparison is enough for one function.
Maybe temperature characterization must be repeated.
Maybe firmware and calibration change together.
Maybe a small pilot lot is appropriate before broad cut-in.

Once the evidence is adequate for the intended decision, the team releases the relevant definitions and records where the alternate becomes effective.

Now, if a field issue appears later, it can separate units with sensor A from units with sensor B and ask whether the signal follows the actual configuration.

That is when configuration management stops feeling like administration and starts feeling like engineering leverage.

---

## 9. When lightweight tools stop being enough

A spreadsheet can be excellent configuration management for a five-person team.

Until it is not.

The trigger for more formal tooling is not that somebody saw a mature company using PLM.

It is that the information relationships are breaking.

You have multiple builders and nobody knows which BOM is current.

Suppliers receive different revisions.

Old and new components coexist in WIP.

Firmware and hardware combinations drift.

Test stations are running different software.

Changes are approved but cut in incorrectly.

Field units cannot be reconstructed.

Engineers spend hours reconciling spreadsheets before every investigation.

At that point, better integrated tooling may be worth the cost.

But remember what the tool is supposed to preserve:

Definition.
Actual configuration.
Change history.
Effectivity.
Evidence.

Buying software before those relationships are understood just digitizes ambiguity.

---

## 10. DEV, LVP and serial production

In DEV, the minimum goal is simple:

Know what configuration produced each important engineering result.

A serial label, BOM snapshot, drawing revision, firmware commit and test record may be enough.

In low-volume production, configuration becomes production infrastructure.

You now need controlled product definitions, clearer change authority, effectivity, supplier lots where relevant, deviations and rework, programming and calibration identity, and enough as-built history to learn from repeated builds.

In serial production, the same discipline has to survive scale.

More suppliers.
More operators.
More sites.
More software versions.
More field units.
More changes.

Now genealogy and configuration history allow you to answer questions like:

Which population is affected?

Did the failure begin after a supplier or process change?

Which firmware was paired with which hardware?

Which units were reworked?

What evidence supports the configuration currently in the field?

Serial production does not mean the product stops changing.

It means the production system can absorb change without losing historical truth.

---

## 11. The Five-Build Reconstruction Challenge

Here is the practical action for this episode.

Take the last five meaningful prototypes or builds your team produced.

Do not ask the original builder first.

Try to reconstruct each one from the records you already have.

Which mechanical revision?

Which PCB and BOM?

Which firmware?

Which important material or supplier state?

Which calibration or configuration?

Which test procedure?

What were the results?

Was there any deviation or rework?

If a field problem appeared tomorrow, could you separate these five units correctly?

Every missing fact is not automatically a disaster.

But every missing fact that could change an engineering conclusion is configuration debt.

Use what you learn to create a Minimum Prototype Configuration Record for the next build.

Start small.

Add fields only when the product, risk or production system proves that they matter.

---

## Closing

We started this season with an idea.

Then we made requirements explicit.
Mapped ownership.
Defined architecture and interfaces.
Chose prototypes based on what they needed to teach us.
Built mechanical and electronics evidence.
Planned verification before test activity became expensive.

And now we have added the final foundation:

Preserve the identity of the thing that generated the evidence.

Because a test report without a reconstructable configuration is much weaker than it looks.

A change without effectivity is much less controlled than it looks.

A final PASS without rework history may be much less informative than it looks.

And a PLM system full of poorly defined relationships is not configuration management just because the software is expensive.

The goal is engineering truth that survives time and change.

From here, the series moves into the transition that catches many hardware teams by surprise.

The product works.

Maybe customers like it.
Maybe engineering is proud of it.

So why can't we just manufacture it?

That is where Episode 1 begins.
